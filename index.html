Meet SAW, the Socially Assistive Walker

SAW looks like an ordinary wheeled walker, but it can sense how you are walking and talk back. Sensors near the floor follow your steps, the handles feel how you lean on them, and a camera checks whether you are standing, leaning or sitting. The walker then helps in gentle ways: lights on its front and a gentle buzz in its handles show which way to go, it applies its brakes before you sit down, and a friendly face on its screen reminds you about everyday tasks such as taking medication. Older adults and carers helped design it, so that people can keep walking safely and independently at home.

Try the walker at the exhibition

At the museum the walker runs a guided tour on its own screen. It works offline: nothing you say and no camera image is recorded or sent anywhere. You do not need to press anything to start, but you are welcome to try these:

  1. Watch and listen

    The tour plays by itself, with a spoken voice and the lights on the front of the walker. Each part shows one thing the walker can do: its friendly face, daily tasks, walking, the laser, posture, directions and safety.

  2. Jump to a part

    Tap one of the tiles at the bottom of the screen to go straight to that part of the tour. Restart goes back to the beginning.

  3. Stand behind the walker

    In the Walking and Laser parts, stand between the handles and walk on the spot. The laser near the floor finds your legs and shows them on the screen, live. Step back or to one side and watch the lights change.

  4. Strike a pose

    In the Posture part, stand in front of the walker. The camera finds your shoulders: lean to one side, then raise both hands to say hello. The picture is only shown on the screen, never stored.

  5. Talk to me

    Tap Talk to me. When the screen says I'm listening… ask me a question, ask a short question in English, such as What can you do? or Who made you?. It answers out loud. Tap Ask another question to keep chatting. The walker only understands English for now.

  6. Make it easier to follow

    Use A− and A+ for smaller or bigger text, and the speaker button to mute or unmute the voice.

Please remember

  • This walker is a research prototype for display. Please do not lean on it, sit on it or use it as a walking aid.
  • Please do not touch the cables, the laser near the floor or the camera.
  • Something not working? Please tell a member of staff. Staff can find help in the staff guide.

How it works

Every feature below is taken from the walker's real software. The walker senses, a small computer thinks, and the walker responds with light, touch, voice, its screen or its brakes. Tap Next on any picture to step through it yourself.

The whole system: sense, think, respond

Example

  1. User LiDAR finds your legs → if you drift out of the comfort zone, the front lights change from green to yellow.
  2. Handle force sensors notice you pressing much harder on one side → the walker says “Try to keep a gentle, even pressure through both hands.”
  3. Camera sees you have sat down → the companion invites you to chat.
  4. IMU and wheel encoders track where you are → on a saved route, the light on one side and the vibration in that handle say “turn this way”, and the voice says “Turn left”.
  5. Front LiDAR checks the way ahead → like a parking sensor, the blocked side lights up red.
  6. Before you sit down to rest → the walker applies its brakes and tells you on screen.

How it works

A 2D LiDAR near the floor faces you and scans about ten times a second. The software groups the laser points into clusters (DBSCAN), keeps the two that look like legs, and follows each one with its own Kalman filter, so a leg briefly hidden behind the other is not lost.

Each time a foot passes the other, a step is counted. From the steps it calculates step length, cadence (steps per minute) and walking speed, and these feed a gait report on the screen.

It also compares where your legs are with a comfort zone behind the walker and reports too far, too close, off centre or legs crossing. During a walk, the front lights stay green when legs, posture and handle pressure are all fine, and turn more yellow the more of them are off.

Sense Handles that feel your weight

Balanced Even pressure on both handles.

How it works

Each handle has force-sensing resistors (FSRs). Their readings are averaged per handle and published left and right, many times a second.

The walker compares the two sides with a balance index, the difference divided by the total, |R − L| / (R + L). Up to 0.2 counts as balanced. If both hands come off, it knows nobody is holding on.

During a guided walk, if one side carries clearly more weight, the walker gently says: Try to keep a gentle, even pressure through both hands. It waits a while before repeating itself, so it does not nag.

Sense Knowing where it has been

Tracking Distance from the wheels, direction from the IMU.

How it works

Encoders on the rear wheels count tiny steps of rotation, which tells the walker how far it has rolled. An IMU (a BNO08x motion sensor, fused with a Madgwick filter) gives the direction it is facing.

The walker adds up “this far, in this direction” many times a second to estimate its position. This is called odometry. It lets the walker record a route, for example from the sofa to the kitchen, and guide you back along it later.

Sense Standing, leaning or seated?

Standing Shoulders level and at the calibrated height.

How it works

A camera sits at hip height on the walker and looks up at you. Body-tracking software (MediaPipe) finds your shoulders, and the walker compares them with your own calibrated upright posture:

  • Tilted left or right: the shoulder line rotates.
  • Leaning forward: your shoulders look wider because they are closer, and your feet (seen by the laser) are further back.
  • Seated: your shoulders drop well below their standing height.

When the walker sees you sit down after a walk, the companion can start a conversation with you.

Respond Lights, vibration and brakes

How it works

An ESP32-S3 microcontroller on a Raspberry Pi shield handles the fast, physical jobs. Its firmware runs five real-time (FreeRTOS) tasks: the two LED strips on the front of the frame (20 LEDs each, any colour and brightness), the vibration motors in the left and right handles (pulsing 200 ms on, 200 ms off), the two electro-mechanical brakes, two servo outputs, and the wheel encoders.

The Pi talks to it with short text commands, like the ones shown above, and the ESP32 streams back the encoder counts. Its firmware can even be updated over Wi-Fi.

The brakes are used when safety matters: before you sit down in a resting session the walker says “The brakes are being engaged.”, and in a fall-detection demonstration it applies the brakes, turns the lights red and asks “Are you okay?”.

How it works

The tablet runs a web app connected to the walker's ROS software. It shows a face that blinks, moves its mouth while speaking, and can look neutral, happy, sad, concerned or surprised. Speech is spoken out loud through the walker's speaker, and the walker can listen through a microphone.

Micro-prompting breaks everyday activities into small, clear steps, such as taking medication, a timed walk, or sitting down safely for a rest, with large text and a big NEXT button. The walker can also hold a short conversation using a large language model.

Respond Showing the way with light and touch

Go straight Both handles green.

How it works

From the Directions menu you can walk a route once, such as to the kitchen or bedroom, and the walker records it with odometry. Later it guides you along the saved path.

Instructions are given three ways at once: the front lights (both green for Go straight, only the left one for Turn left, only the right one for Turn right, amber for Turn around, red for Stop, obstacle ahead, blue when you have arrived), short spoken instructions, and the vibration motor in the handle on the side you should turn towards.

In obstacles mode the front laser works like a car parking sensor, and the blocked side of the walker lights up red.

Think Inside: Raspberry Pi and ROS

Tablet

Web app (Angular): companion face, micro-prompts, directions, gait report, settings. Connects to ROS through a WebSocket (roslib).

Raspberry Pi running ROS

Many small programs (“nodes”) that pass messages to each other:

  • leg tracking & gait
  • posture
  • odometry
  • navigation assist
  • behaviours & demos
  • speech in / out
  • conversation
  • user profiles

I²C sensors

IMU and handle force sensors, shared over MQTT.

USB sensors

2D LiDARs (RPLIDAR) and camera.

ESP32-S3

Serial link: lights, vibration, brakes, encoders.

ROS, the Robot Operating System, is free software used by robot builders worldwide. It lets each feature be built and tested on its own and then connected together.
